An oil well is a declining annuity you paid for in advance

Everything peculiar about the oil business follows from that. You spend the money first, the production arrives on a curve that falls steeply and never stops falling, the price you receive is set by a global market and a handful of governments, and the only way to stay the same size is to keep drilling. This guide starts at zero and works through the whole chain — the rock, the well, the decline curve, the barrel's journey to a refinery, the economics, the accounting, the fiscal regimes, the promotional tricks, and the map as it stands in a year the Strait of Hormuz has been effectively closed since February.

01  /  Orientation

What an oil company actually is

A business that spends capital to convert an underground asset into a stream of cash that begins high, falls fast, and requires continuous reinvestment merely to stand still.

Oil shares one deep property with mining — the asset depletes as you produce it — and then diverges from it almost immediately. In a mine, the metal sits still and you go and get it; production rate is a choice made by the size of the plant. In an oil field, the hydrocarbon flows to you under pressure, and the rate at which it does so is set by physics you influence but do not control. That single difference produces the decline curve, which is the organising fact of the entire industry.

Where the opening line breaks

The declining-annuity line is a heuristic for a single shale well, not a law of the industry. Three things make the payments less fixed than an annuity's. Prices move, so the same barrels can be worth half or double. Operators have discretion: choke management, refracs, shut-ins and workovers all change the curve. And the volumes are estimates; conventional fields on waterflood or with strong pressure support can hold a plateau for years before declining. At company level the line also understates choice, because a producer can stop drilling and harvest its decline for cash.

A modern shale well might produce 1,200 barrels a day in its first month and 300 barrels a day two years later. Nobody made that decision. Reservoir pressure fell, the fractures that connect the rock to the wellbore drained the volume nearest them, and the rate collapsed accordingly. Roughly half of a shale well's total lifetime output arrives within its first three years. Everything about how these companies are financed, valued and mismanaged follows from that shape.

The consequence for a company is the treadmill. If your base production declines at 30% a year, you must replace 30% of your output every year through new drilling just to report flat volumes. Growth requires replacing considerably more than that. This is why "free cash flow" was, for a decade, something the American shale industry discussed rather than produced, and why capital discipline became the sector's defining argument after 2020.

The spine of the business

Source rock → migration → trap → reservoir → lease → well → flow rate → decline curve → gross production → royalty and tax deductions → net revenue → operating costs → cash flow → reserve replacement → value.

Two links on that chain have no equivalent in mining. Lease sits early because in the United States, uniquely among major producers, private individuals own the minerals beneath their land — which created a royalty industry, a leasing industry and a body of law that shapes how every American well is financed. And decline curve sits in the middle because it converts a single capital decision into a fixed, physically determined revenue schedule.

Four things that make oil unlike other industries

1

The asset destroys itself on a schedule

Not by management choice. Decline is a physical property of the reservoir, and it sets the reinvestment rate the business must sustain forever.

2

The price is partly administered

A group of governments has historically withheld several million barrels a day of capacity from the market. No industrial commodity has an equivalent. When that group fragments — as it did in 2026 — the price distribution changes shape.

3

The product is not fungible

"Oil" is hundreds of crudes with different densities and sulphur contents, each worth a different amount to each refinery. What you receive at the wellhead can differ from the screen price by ten dollars or more.

4

The tax code was built to encourage it

Intangible drilling costs, percentage depletion and production-sharing cost recovery are not loopholes discovered by clever accountants. They are deliberate policy, and they materially change the after-tax economics of every well.

02  /  Petroleum geology

Why the oil is there, and why it stayed

A conventional oil field requires five independent things to have gone right. Miss one and there is nothing to find.

Oil is cooked plankton. Organic matter — mostly marine algae and plankton, occasionally terrestrial plant material — settles into an oxygen-poor basin where it is buried rather than eaten. Over millions of years, burial subjects it to heat and pressure, and the organic material converts to kerogen and then, in a specific temperature window, to liquid hydrocarbons. Too cool and nothing happens. Too hot and you get natural gas, and then, hotter still, nothing usable at all.

1

Source rock

Organic-rich shale or marl that contains enough kerogen to generate hydrocarbons. Total organic carbon of 2% or more is generally required; the best sources run far higher.

2

Maturation

The oil window sits roughly between 60°C and 120°C, typically two to four kilometres deep depending on the local geothermal gradient. Above it, immature kerogen. Below it, the gas window and eventually over-mature barren rock.

3

Migration

Oil is less dense than the water saturating the rock, so it moves upward through permeable conduits until something stops it. Most generated oil never gets trapped and leaks to surface over geological time.

4

Reservoir

Rock with porosity — void space to hold fluid — and permeability — connected pores that let fluid flow. Sandstones and carbonates typically. Porosity is storage; permeability is deliverability, and permeability is what actually pays you.

5

Seal and trap

An impermeable barrier (shale, salt, anhydrite) in a geometry that holds the oil: an anticline, a fault block, a salt dome flank, a stratigraphic pinch-out, a reef. The trap must also have existed before migration, which is where many prospects fail.

6

Preservation

Everything above, followed by tens of millions of years without the trap being breached, tilted, biodegraded or cooked. The unconformable truth of exploration is that most complete petroleum systems produce nothing.

The unconventional revolution, stated precisely

Shale changed the industry by removing two of the five requirements. In a shale play, the source rock is the reservoir. There is no migration and no trap — the oil never left the rock that generated it, which is why shale is often called a source-rock or resource play rather than a field. What makes it work is that the mature source interval is laterally extensive and continuous over enormous areas. You are not hunting for a trap; you know where the oil is over thousands of square miles.

The problem is permeability. Shale permeability is measured in nanodarcies — vanishingly small, hundreds of thousands of times tighter than a conventional reservoir. Oil in shale will not flow to a wellbore in economic quantity by itself. Two technologies solved that in combination: horizontal drilling, which places thousands of feet of wellbore inside a thin productive interval, and hydraulic fracturing, which creates artificial permeability by cracking the rock and propping the cracks open with sand.

The consequence nobody anticipated. This converted exploration risk into manufacturing risk. A conventional wildcat either finds a field or does not — a binary outcome with perhaps a one-in-five success rate. A shale well almost always produces something; the question is whether it produces enough, at a cost low enough, to earn a return. The industry stopped being a search and became a repeatable industrial process with a cost curve. That is why shale responds to price in months where conventional projects respond in years, and why it briefly made the United States the marginal supplier to the world.

03  /  Reserves and reporting

Reserves are an accounting construct with a geological input

The number on the cover of an annual report is the output of a pricing rule, a five-year clock and an engineer's signature — not a measurement of oil.

Mining separates resources from reserves by economic testing. Oil does something similar and gives it different names, with one crucial addition: American reserve reporting is governed by a rule that fixes the price you must use, which means reported reserves move when prices move even if not one molecule of geology has changed.

Three rules that catch people out

SEC

The 12-month average price rule

Since 2009, US filers must book reserves using the unweighted arithmetic average of the first-day-of-month price for the preceding twelve months, held flat. Not the strip, not a forecast. Reserves therefore swell in a rising market and get written down in a falling one, on the same rock. Every year-end reserve headline is partly a price story.

SEC

The five-year PUD rule

A proved undeveloped location may generally only stay on the books if it is scheduled to be drilled within five years of first booking, supported by an adopted development plan the company has the capital and intent to execute. Booking a deep inventory of PUDs is easy; keeping it is not, and PUD revisions are among the more honest signals a company emits.

Who signs

Reserve auditors

Independent engineering firms — Netherland Sewell, DeGolyer & MacNaughton, Ryder Scott, Sproule, GLJ — prepare or audit reserve reports. There is no single named individual with personal liability equivalent to mining's Qualified Person, and the distinction between a full independent evaluation and a lighter audit or process review is worth reading in the footnote.

Outside the US, most reporting follows the SPE/WPC/AAPG/SPEE Petroleum Resources Management System (PRMS), which is probabilistic and forward-price based. Canada adds National Instrument 51-101, which requires forecast-price reserve reports and an independent evaluator. The two systems will give materially different numbers for the same asset — a fact that recurs in cross-border deal disputes.

The numbers derived from reserves

Standardized measure and PV-10 are the discounted present values of proved reserves computed under the SEC's rules, after and before income tax respectively, at a mandated 10% discount rate. Neither is a valuation. Both use a flat historical price, ignore probable reserves, ignore any well not yet booked as a PUD, and use a discount rate chosen by regulation rather than by the market. Analysts still quote them constantly, because they are the only reserve-based figures that are computed identically across every US filer.

Reserve replacement ratio is reserves added in a year divided by production in that year. Above 100% means the company grew its booked inventory. It is easily flattered: acquisitions count, and so do positive price revisions that arrive purely because the 12-month average rose. Insist on the organic, price-neutral number, which companies do disclose if you find the reconciliation table.

Reserves are not inventory in the retail sense. They are the subset of hydrocarbon that a specific pricing rule, a specific development plan and a specific engineer's judgment agree is recoverable. Change the price rule and the number changes. Change management's stated intent to drill and the number changes. This is not fraud; it is how the system is designed. The error is treating the output as a physical fact.
04  /  Drilling and completion

How a well gets made

Two operations with different costs, different contractors and different failure modes — and completion is now the larger and more consequential half.

A well is drilled and then it is completed, and the industry keeps the two words separate because they are separate businesses. Drilling creates the hole. Completion turns the hole into a producing conduit. In a modern shale well, completion accounts for roughly 55 to 70% of total well cost, which reverses the intuition most newcomers bring.

Drilling, in sequence

1

Spud and surface hole

The rig begins drilling. A large-diameter surface section is drilled and lined with steel casing cemented in place, isolating and protecting shallow freshwater aquifers. Regulators care about this section more than any other.

2

Intermediate section

Drilling continues through zones of unstable rock or abnormal pressure, each isolated with further casing strings. Drilling mud circulates to carry cuttings up, cool the bit and — critically — hold back formation pressure. Losing that balance is how blowouts happen.

3

The curve and the lateral

The bit is steered from vertical through a build section into horizontal, then drilled along the target interval. Laterals of 10,000 feet are routine; 15,000 and even 20,000 feet are increasingly common. Longer laterals spread fixed costs over more rock, which is where much of the last decade's cost improvement came from.

4

Total depth and casing

Production casing is run and cemented across the lateral. The rig then moves on — often to a neighbouring well on the same pad, since moving a rig a hundred feet is far cheaper than moving it thirty miles.

Completion, in sequence

A separate crew, separate equipment and separate money. The lateral is perforated in stages — dozens of them along a long lateral. Each stage is isolated and injected with water, chemicals and proppant (sand, mostly) at pressures high enough to crack the rock. The proppant lodges in the cracks and holds them open when pressure is released, creating the artificial permeability the reservoir lacks. Then the well is flowed back, the frac water returns, and production begins.

The variables that drive results are proppant loading per foot of lateral, fluid volume per foot, stage spacing and cluster design. "Bigger fracs" raised early rates enormously through the late 2010s. They also, in many cases, drained the same rock faster rather than draining more of it — which shows up as a steeper decline and a similar ultimate recovery, at higher cost.

Parent-child, and why infill drilling disappoints. When a new well is drilled beside an existing producer, the older "parent" has already depleted pressure in the rock around it. The new "child" well frequently underperforms the parent, sometimes badly, and can damage the parent's production as well. Companies drill their best, most isolated locations first, so as a field develops the average well gets worse for reasons that have nothing to do with technique. Any type curve built on early parent wells and applied to a dense infill programme is overstating the future. This is the single most important technical criticism of shale inventory claims.

Vocabulary that recurs in earnings calls

DUC

Drilled but uncompleted well. Cheap optionality — the drilling capital is already spent, so bringing a DUC online costs only completion money. A rising DUC count means capital has been spent without production arriving; a falling one flatters near-term volumes.

Spud-to-TD and cycle time

Days from starting the hole to reaching total depth, and the full cycle to first production. Falling cycle times are the clearest measure of genuine operational improvement — and they are also why fewer rigs now produce the same volume.

Pad drilling

Multiple wells drilled from a single surface location. Reduces facility, road and permitting cost per well, and enables simultaneous completion of several wells ("zipper fracs") that improves stimulation. Also means production arrives in lumps rather than continuously.

Spacing

The distance between laterals and between stacked benches. Too wide and you leave oil behind; too tight and wells cannibalise each other. Spacing assumptions determine claimed location counts, and location counts determine inventory life, and inventory life determines the valuation. Follow the chain.

Frac hits

A completion in one well communicating with a nearby producer, sometimes damaging it permanently. A real operating cost that appears in nobody's cost metric.

Day rate versus turnkey

How the drilling contractor is paid. Day rate places geological and mechanical risk on the operator; turnkey places it on the contractor at a higher price. Offshore day rates for high-specification rigs are the most cyclical price in the service sector.

05  /  Production and decline

The curve that governs everything

One equation, published in 1945, still does most of the forecasting work in the oil industry.

J.J. Arps observed that oil wells decline in mathematically regular ways and described three cases. Exponential decline falls by a constant percentage each period. Harmonic decline flattens quickly. Hyperbolic decline sits between them and fits most real wells, especially unconventional ones:

q(t) = qₐ ÷ (1 + b · Dₐ · t)^(1/b)

Three parameters do all the work. qₐ is the initial rate. Dₐ is the initial decline rate. b is the hyperbolic exponent that controls how quickly the decline itself decelerates — b = 0 gives pure exponential, b = 1 gives harmonic, and shale wells are commonly fitted with b between 0.8 and 1.5. That b-factor is the most consequential and most contested number in shale valuation, because a small increase in b adds a very long tail of low-rate production and therefore a great deal of claimed ultimate recovery.

Left alone, a hyperbolic curve with b above 1 produces infinite cumulative recovery, which is obviously wrong. Standard practice is to impose a terminal decline — typically 5 to 8% a year — and switch to exponential once the hyperbolic decline rate falls that low. Whether an operator applies a terminal decline, and at what rate, is a question worth asking of any type curve.

Type curves, and the leap of faith inside them

A company does not forecast each well individually. It builds a type curve — an average expected profile for a class of wells in a defined area — and multiplies it by a location count. Both halves of that multiplication deserve scrutiny. The type curve is usually fitted to the wells drilled so far, which are the best wells, drilled in the best rock, at wide spacing. The location count depends on an assumed spacing that has repeatedly proved too tight in practice.

The honest way to read a type curve is to ask: which wells is this fitted to, over what period, at what spacing, with what b-factor and terminal decline, and how does the most recent vintage of wells actually compare? Operators publish enough to answer that. Very few investors ask.

Recovery factor, and why so much stays underground

Conventional oil fields under natural drive recover perhaps 20 to 35% of the oil in place; with waterflooding and gas injection, good fields reach 40 to 60%. Shale recovers something in the range of 5 to 10%. The rock holds enormous volumes and gives up very little of it, which is simultaneously the reason shale inventories look so large and the reason enhanced recovery in shale has been an area of persistent, so far mostly disappointing, experimentation.

Conventional reservoirs also age differently. Water encroaches, the water cut rises, and eventually a well produces fifty barrels of water for every barrel of oil and is shut in — not because the oil ran out but because handling the water costs more than the oil is worth. Gas-oil ratio rises as reservoir pressure drops below the bubble point and dissolved gas comes out of solution. Both are worth tracking in any producing asset: they are the clock.

06  /  From wellhead to pump

The barrel's journey, and where its value goes

The price you see on a screen is not the price a producer receives. The gap between them is a business.

When a headline says oil is $101, it means a specific futures contract for a specific grade delivered at a specific place. Brent is light sweet crude priced for waterborne delivery in the North Sea. WTI is light sweet crude delivered at Cushing, Oklahoma. Dubai and Oman price the medium sour barrels that flow east. A producer in the Permian receives WTI at Cushing minus the cost and constraint of getting the barrel there, adjusted for whether the crude is exactly the specified quality.

Differentials, and what they are telling you

A differential is the price gap between a local crude and a benchmark. It is composed of three things: the physical cost of transport, the quality difference, and — most informatively — scarcity of takeaway capacity. When Permian production outran pipeline capacity in 2018, the Midland differential blew out to more than $17 under WTI. When Canadian production outran export capacity, Western Canadian Select traded $50 under WTI at moments in 2018. Both are the market pricing a bottleneck, and both were repaired by pipelines.

This is why firm transportation — a contractual right to space on a pipeline, paid for whether used or not — is a genuine competitive asset, and why midstream companies with long-term contracted volumes are valued more like infrastructure than like energy.

Refining, in one page

A refinery buys crude and sells products. Its margin is the crack spread — the value of the product slate minus the cost of the crude. The conventional shorthand is the 3-2-1 crack: three barrels of crude yielding two of gasoline and one of distillate. Distillate — diesel and jet — has been the tight product through 2026, with US inventories forecast to fall below 100 million barrels and stay under the five-year low well into 2027.

The economically interesting property of refining is that complexity pays. A simple refinery can only process light sweet crude into a modest product slate. A complex refinery with a coker and a hydrocracker can buy cheap heavy sour crude and upgrade it into the same high-value products, capturing the quality differential as margin. This is why the light-heavy spread matters enormously to refiners and to the producers of heavy crude, and why sanctions that reroute particular grades create such uneven winners.

Contango, backwardation and floating storage. When futures trade above spot (contango), storing oil pays: you buy physical, sell forward, and collect the spread minus storage cost. When futures trade below spot (backwardation), holding inventory costs money and the market is telling you it wants barrels now. A steeply backwardated curve is one of the more reliable signals of genuine physical tightness — and the 2026 curve has spent most of the year in it.
07  /  Economics

The well works. Does the company?

Shale's central paradox: an industry of individually attractive projects that collectively consumed more capital than it returned for over a decade.

Run the model in section 5 at almost any price above $60 and a good Permian well looks excellent — payout inside two years, an internal rate of return north of 40%. Yet between roughly 2010 and 2019 the US shale sector as a whole generated persistently negative free cash flow. Both facts are true, and reconciling them is the most useful thing an investor can learn about the business.

Half-cycle versus full-cycle

The well economics above are half-cycle: they count the cost of drilling and completing one well against the revenue that one well produces. They exclude the price paid for the acreage, the seismic and appraisal work that identified it, corporate overhead, the interest on the debt that funded it, the infrastructure built to move the production, and the eventual cost of plugging the well.

Full-cycle economics include all of it. The gap is large. A well showing a 45% half-cycle return can sit inside a company that never earns its cost of capital, because acreage was bought at a price that assumed those returns, and because the returns were reinvested into more wells rather than returned to owners.

The treadmill, in numbers

A company producing 100,000 barrels a day with a 35% corporate base decline must add 35,000 barrels a day of new production every year to report flat volumes. If each new well delivers 400 barrels a day in its first year, that is roughly 88 wells a year at, say, $8.5 million each — about $750 million of annual capital before any growth, before overhead, and before a dollar reaches a shareholder.

Now cut the price by a third. Cash flow falls faster than capital requirements do, because the decline rate is a property of the rock rather than of the price. This is the mechanism by which shale companies got into trouble, twice.

The metrics that connect a well to a company

1

F&D cost

Finding and development cost per barrel: capital spent divided by reserves added. The denominator is the problem — it can include acquisitions, price-driven revisions and optimistic PUD bookings. Ask for the three-year average, organic only, on a drill-bit basis.

2

Recycle ratio

Netback per barrel divided by F&D cost per barrel. Above 2.0x is generally considered healthy; below 1.0x means the company is destroying value with every barrel it replaces. The single most compressed measure of whether an E&P works.

3

Maintenance capital

The capital required to hold production flat. Rarely disclosed directly and easily estimated: base decline multiplied by production, divided by the first-year output of a typical new well, times well cost. Free cash flow above maintenance capital is the number that matters.

4

Corporate base decline

The blended decline of everything the company already has producing. A conventional producer might run 6 to 10%; a shale-heavy one 30 to 40%. It determines the size of the treadmill and it is disclosed by good operators.

5

Inventory life

Remaining drilling locations divided by the annual drilling pace. Ten years of Tier 1 inventory supports a premium; three years does not, regardless of current cash flow. Interrogate the spacing and the tier definitions behind the location count.

6

Reinvestment rate

Capital expenditure as a percentage of operating cash flow. Above 100% the company is outspending; the post-2020 discipline consensus settled around 50 to 70%, with the balance going to dividends, buybacks and debt reduction. Watch for it creeping back up when prices rise.

Offshore and conventional: a different shape entirely

A deepwater project inverts nearly every parameter. Capital is measured in billions rather than millions and is committed five to eight years before first oil. The decline is shallow — perhaps 5 to 10% a year — and the field produces for two or three decades. There is no ability to stop halfway, no meaningful optionality once the platform is ordered, and the entire economic case rests on a price forecast made a decade out.

The trade-off is stark: shale is short-cycle, low-margin per barrel, and can be turned off; deepwater is long-cycle, high-margin per barrel, and cannot. A portfolio of both is what an integrated major is actually selling. The lowest-cost barrels in the world, meanwhile, are neither — they are the conventional onshore fields of the Middle East, where lifting costs run in the low single digits per barrel and the constraint has always been political rather than economic.

Free cash flowOperating cash flow less all capital expenditure.The only metric that cannot be defined away. Distorted for a year or two by underinvestment or by DUC drawdown; over a full cycle it is decisive.
Successful efforts versus full cost. Two permitted accounting methods for capitalising exploration. Under successful efforts, dry hole costs are expensed immediately. Under full cost, they are capitalised into a pool and depleted against production regardless of whether the individual well worked. Full cost reports smoother, higher earnings in bad years and carries a mandatory ceiling test that forces large, sudden write-downs when prices fall. Two identical companies on different methods will report materially different earnings and book value, and neither is lying.
The cost that appears nowhere. Every well must eventually be plugged and the site restored. Asset retirement obligations sit on the balance sheet at a discounted estimate that is frequently below true cost, particularly for old, low-rate stripper wells whose owners have no capital. Orphan wells have become a significant public liability in Texas, Oklahoma, Pennsylvania and Alberta. When an E&P sells its declining assets to a small private buyer, ask who ends up holding that obligation.
09  /  Valuation

Putting a number on a declining stream

Every approach is a discounted cash flow underneath. The differences are in which barrels you count and what price you assume.

The base method is net asset value: model each producing property's decline curve, add risked value for undeveloped locations, discount at 10%, subtract debt and capitalised overhead. In practice analysts build this in tiers — proved developed producing, proved undeveloped, then an increasingly discounted stack of probable, possible and unbooked inventory. Where a company's share price sits within that stack tells you exactly what the market is paying for.

PV-10 / SEC NAV

Standardised and comparable across US filers, and wrong on purpose — flat historical pricing, proved only. Use it as a floor and a comparability tool, never as a target price.

Strip NAV

The same model run at the forward futures curve rather than a flat price. Closer to what the asset could actually be hedged at, and the number most transactions are negotiated against.

EV/EBITDAX

The X is exploration expense, added back so successful-efforts and full-cost companies can be compared. A blunt instrument that ignores reserve life entirely — a 4x multiple on a five-year asset is expensive.

EV per flowing barrel

Enterprise value divided by current daily production. Fast, crude, and everywhere in deal commentary. Says nothing about decline rate, inventory depth or netback, which is to say it says nothing about the three things that matter.

Hedging, and reading through it

Producers hedge to protect a drilling programme or to satisfy a lender, using swaps that fix a price, collars that bound a range, or puts that buy a floor. The consequence for an analyst is that realised price in a quarterly report may be substantially above or below the market price, and that difference is a financial position rather than an operating result. Strip the hedge gains out before comparing operating performance across companies or quarters. A company whose reported margins depend on hedges placed two years ago is telling you about its treasury desk, not its rock.

What actually drives an E&P share price

In descending order of practical importance: the oil price, the oil price, the inventory depth and quality behind current production, the base decline rate, the balance sheet, and the credibility of management's capital allocation. The first item dominates so heavily that many investors treat E&P equity as a leveraged commodity position with an operational tracking error — which is unkind, largely accurate, and the reason royalty companies and integrated majors trade at premiums to pure-play producers.

10  /  Ownership and financing

Who owns the barrel

American oil rests on a legal peculiarity: private ownership of subsurface minerals. Almost nowhere else works this way, and it explains most of the industry's structure.

In the United States, whoever owns the mineral estate beneath a piece of land owns the hydrocarbons in it. Everywhere else of consequence — Saudi Arabia, Norway, Brazil, Nigeria, the United Kingdom, Canada's Crown lands — the state owns the subsurface and grants companies the right to extract. That single difference produced the American landman, the lease bonus, the mineral royalty industry, and a sector where thousands of small private owners each hold a slice of the resource.

A partner with a 50% working interest and a 37.5% NRI pays half of every cost and receives 37.5% of gross revenue. Confusing the two overstates cash flow by a third, and it is a mistake that appears in real models with real money behind them.

How wells get funded

Debt

Reserve-based lending

The defining credit instrument of US independents. A bank syndicate lends against a borrowing base — a percentage of the discounted value of proved reserves — recalculated twice a year at the banks' own price deck. When prices fall, borrowing bases are cut, and companies must repay just as cash flow collapses. This procyclicality is why the 2015 and 2020 downturns produced so many bankruptcies so quickly.

Structured

DrillCo and non-op capital

A financial partner funds a defined drilling programme in exchange for a large working interest until it reaches an agreed return, after which its interest reverts sharply downward. Expensive capital that does not appear as debt — which is exactly why it appealed to companies with strained balance sheets.

Structured

Volumetric production payments

Selling a fixed volume of future production for cash today. A stream by another name, and treated in law as a sale of a real property interest rather than a loan — which has had consequences in bankruptcy that surprised several lenders.

Equity

Minerals and royalty companies

Texas Pacific Land, Viper Energy, Sitio, Black Stone. They buy royalty and mineral interests, bear no drilling or operating cost, and let the operators around them fund growth. The oil sector's closest analogue to mining's Franco-Nevada, and they trade at similar premiums for similar reasons.

Equity

MLPs

Master limited partnerships, mostly midstream, which avoid entity-level tax by distributing nearly all cash flow and issuing K-1s rather than 1099s. The structure suits stable fee-based pipelines and suits volatile upstream production badly, which the upstream MLP experiment demonstrated conclusively.

Contract

The JOA

The joint operating agreement governs every multi-party well: who operates, how costs are billed, what happens when a partner will not fund its share (the "non-consent" penalty, which can run to several hundred percent of the unpaid amount before the partner's interest reverts). Unglamorous, and decisive when a deal goes wrong.

11  /  Fiscal regimes and tax

How the government takes its share

Roughly two-thirds of the world's oil is produced under a fiscal regime that never gives the company title to a barrel. The rest is taxed conventionally, and the US taxes it favourably.

The two great families

A concession — also called a royalty/tax regime — grants the company the right to produce and to own the hydrocarbons at the wellhead. The state takes a royalty on gross production and then taxes the resulting profit. Simple, transparent, and the norm in the United States, Canada, the United Kingdom, Norway, Australia and much of Latin America.

A production sharing contract works differently. The state retains ownership of the resource; the company is a contractor. Production is divided into cost oil, which reimburses the contractor's capital and operating spending up to an annual ceiling, and profit oil, which is split between state and contractor according to a negotiated formula — often sliding with cumulative production, the contractor's return to date, or an R-factor measuring revenue against cost. The contractor then pays income tax on its profit oil. PSCs dominate in Indonesia, Malaysia, Egypt, Nigeria, Angola, Azerbaijan, Iraqi Kurdistan and much of Asia and Africa.

A third family, the service contract, pays the contractor a fee per barrel with no exposure to price at all. Iraq's post-2009 technical service contracts are the largest example. Low risk, low reward, and the reason several majors have quietly reduced their Iraqi exposure.

United States: the most generous upstream tax code in the developed world

1

Intangible drilling costs

Under IRC §263(c), a working interest owner may elect to deduct intangible drilling costs in full in the year incurred rather than capitalising them. IDCs are the non-salvageable portion of a well — labour, rig time, drilling fluids, cementing, fuel, most services — and typically run 60 to 85% of a well's total cost. Tangible equipment is depreciated separately. Integrated producers face partial limits; independents generally do not. The election also creates an AMT preference item and triggers ordinary-income recapture under §1254 on a later sale.

2

Percentage depletion

Independent producers and royalty owners may deduct 15% of gross income from a property, subject to a net income limitation and a production ceiling that excludes the majors. Like its mining equivalent, it is not limited to capital actually invested — a long-producing property can generate cumulative depletion deductions exceeding the original cost basis many times over. This is the reason a mineral royalty is such a tax-efficient asset to hold.

3

Severance and ad valorem taxes

State-level, levied on production value at the wellhead. Texas charges 4.6% on oil and 7.5% on gas; North Dakota, Oklahoma, New Mexico and Wyoming each differ, several with reduced rates for stripper or enhanced-recovery wells. Add county ad valorem property tax on reserves in the ground and the combined state burden commonly runs 6 to 9% of revenue — a real and often-overlooked cost that varies materially by basin.

4

Federal royalty rates

The Inflation Reduction Act of 2022 raised the onshore federal royalty rate from the century-old 12.5% to 16.67% and eliminated non-competitive leasing. The One Big Beautiful Bill Act of July 2025 reversed the rate back toward 12.5% for new leases and expanded lease sale requirements. Federal acreage is a modest share of US production overall and a large share in New Mexico's Delaware Basin, where the change is worth real money.

Why the tax treatment changes behaviour, not just the tax bill. Expensing 80% of a well in year one means a high-bracket working interest participant recovers roughly a third of the investment immediately through tax savings, which lowers the effective at-risk capital and therefore the breakeven. That is the intended policy effect, and it is also why direct drilling programmes are marketed so aggressively to high-income individuals every fourth quarter. The deduction is real. It does not make a bad well good, and the offering documents describing these programmes as tax-advantaged are frequently more rigorous about the tax than about the geology.

Elsewhere

Norway taxes upstream petroleum at 78% — 22% corporate plus a 56% special petroleum tax — but refunds the tax value of exploration costs in cash to companies in a loss position, which is why Norway sustains an unusually active exploration sector despite a punitive headline rate. The United Kingdom layered an Energy Profits Levy onto ring-fenced corporation tax in 2022, pushing the marginal rate to 75% and triggering a visible collapse in North Sea investment commitments. Both illustrate the same principle: what matters is not the headline rate but the interaction of rate, allowances, ring-fencing and loss relief, and a high-rate system with generous uplift can attract more capital than a low-rate system without it.

12  /  Tricks of the trade

The promotional playbook

Oil promotion is less about fabricating a discovery than about presenting a real well as if it were a representative one.

Well results

The 24-hour IP rate

A peak flow rate measured over one day, or even a few hours, and reported as though it characterised the well. Rates can be inflated by flowing back aggressively on a large choke — which damages the reservoir and lowers ultimate recovery. IP30 and IP90 are far more informative, and a company that publishes only the 24-hour number has chosen the least meaningful one available.

Well results

Normalised per-thousand-foot rates

"Best well in the basin on a per-1,000-foot basis" is a construction that lets a mediocre long lateral beat an excellent short one. Normalisation is legitimate for comparing completion designs and misleading for comparing economics, because you paid for the whole lateral.

Forecasting

b-factor inflation

The quietest and most valuable trick in shale. Raising the hyperbolic exponent from 1.0 to 1.4 barely changes the first two years of a forecast and adds a great deal to the twenty-year tail, inflating estimated ultimate recovery and every economic metric derived from it. Ask what b-factor and what terminal decline the type curve uses, and whether the tail has ever actually been observed in that formation.

Forecasting

Type curves fitted to parent wells

Early wells are drilled in the best rock at wide spacing into virgin pressure. Applying their average to a dense infill programme ignores parent-child degradation entirely. Compare the most recent well vintage against the type curve, by year drilled — good operators publish this and the comparison is often unflattering.

Inventory

Location counts built on optimistic spacing

Halving assumed well spacing doubles the location count and doubles claimed inventory life without adding an acre. The industry has repeatedly downgraded spacing assumptions after drilling them. Ask how many of the claimed locations have been tested at the assumed density, and how those tests performed.

Inventory

Tier definitions that move

"Ten years of Tier 1 inventory" is only as good as the company's private definition of Tier 1, which is not standardised and which tends to loosen as the genuinely good rock is consumed. Track the stated breakeven of the inventory over successive investor presentations.

Reserves

Price-driven reserve growth

A 40% increase in proved reserves in a year the company drilled normally is a pricing artefact of the SEC's twelve-month average rule, not a discovery. The reconciliation table separates revisions from extensions, discoveries and acquisitions. Read the table, not the headline.

Reserves

PUDs that never get drilled

Proved undeveloped locations must be scheduled within five years and supported by a plan the company can fund. Persistent PUD removals — as opposed to conversions — mean the inventory was never real. Track the PUD conversion rate over three years.

Reporting

The boe denominator

Reporting growth in barrels of oil equivalent while the mix shifts toward gas is growth in volume and shrinkage in revenue. At $95 oil and $3.50 gas, a boe of gas is worth about a fifth of a boe of oil. Insist on the three-stream split: oil, NGL, gas.

Reporting

Half-cycle breakevens

"We're profitable at $38" almost always means drilling-and-completion cost only, on the best acreage, ignoring land, overhead, interest and the cost of replacing the barrel. Full-cycle corporate breakevens including the dividend are typically twenty to thirty dollars higher.

Financial

Growth funded by the balance sheet

Production growth is trivially easy to buy with debt or equity. Judge it per debt-adjusted share, on a three-year view, alongside the reinvestment rate. A company outspending cash flow to grow volumes is converting shareholder capital into press releases.

Financial

Hedges flattering the operating result

A realised price well above the market means the treasury desk performed, not the assets. Strip hedge gains before comparing margins, and check when the hedge book rolls off — the quarter it does is the quarter you see the real business.

Two questions that do most of the work

Is this half-cycle or full-cycle? Nearly every impressive number in oil promotion is half-cycle. Ask what it excludes and add it back yourself.

What does the most recent vintage look like? Averages across all wells ever drilled flatter a maturing asset. The last twelve months of wells, compared against the type curve they were supposed to deliver, is the honest picture — and it is almost always available.

13  /  Industry structure

Who's who

Listed oil companies are a minority of the industry. The largest producers answer to governments, not shareholders.

Majority of reserves

National oil companies

Saudi Aramco, ADNOC, KPC, NIOC, PetroChina and Sinopec, Petrobras, Pemex, Sonatrach, NNPC, QatarEnergy, Rosneft. Collectively they hold the great majority of the world's proved reserves and the entirety of meaningful spare capacity. Aramco's Ghawar alone has produced more oil than most countries. Any market model that starts with listed companies has already missed most of the supply.

Integrated

The supermajors

ExxonMobil, Chevron, Shell, TotalEnergies, BP, ConocoPhillips (upstream only). Vertically integrated across upstream, refining, chemicals and trading, which smooths the cycle — refining margins tend to widen when crude falls. Exxon absorbed Pioneer for about $60 billion in 2023; Chevron closed its $53 billion Hess acquisition in July 2025, principally for the Guyana position.

Upstream

Large independents

EOG, Occidental, Devon, Diamondback, Coterra, Apache, Expand Energy (gas). Pure upstream, no refining, and therefore an undiluted expression of the commodity. Diamondback paid $26 billion for Endeavor; ConocoPhillips took Marathon for $22.5 billion. The Permian is now dominated by a handful of names in a way it was not five years ago.

Upstream

Small caps and privates

Hundreds of them, plus a large private-equity-backed cohort funded by EnCap, NGP, Quantum and others. The private sector's role is to buy what the majors discard, drill it, and sell it back — and increasingly to operate the mature, low-rate assets nobody else wants, which raises real questions about who ultimately pays to plug them.

Capital

Minerals and royalties

Texas Pacific Land, Viper Energy, Sitio Royalties, Black Stone Minerals, Kimbell. No wells, no costs, no drilling risk — just a perpetual share of whatever the operators around them produce, with percentage depletion sheltering a slice of it. Structurally the best business in oil, priced accordingly.

Capital

Traders

Vitol, Trafigura, Glencore, Gunvor, Mercuria, plus the majors' own desks. They move roughly a third of the world's seaborne crude, arbitrage grade and geography, own storage and refineries, and profit from exactly the dislocation that has defined 2026. Glencore's first-half adjusted EBITDA rose 86%, substantially on energy trading.

Services

Oilfield services

SLB, Halliburton, Baker Hughes, Weatherford, NOV, ProPetro, Liberty. They do the drilling and the fracturing; the operator supplies the acreage and the capital. Their pricing is the operators' cost inflation, which is why service cost deflation assumptions inside a breakeven estimate deserve scepticism.

Services

Drillers and rigs

Transocean, Valaris, Noble, Seadrill offshore; Patterson-UTI, Helmerich & Payne, Nabors onshore. Day rates for high-specification offshore rigs are among the most violently cyclical prices in any industry.

Downstream

Midstream and refiners

Enterprise Products, Energy Transfer, Kinder Morgan, Williams, Plains on the midstream side; Marathon Petroleum, Valero, Phillips 66, Reliance, Shell and Sinopec on refining. Midstream is a toll business valued on contracted volumes; refining is a spread business valued on crack margins and configuration complexity.

The institutions that set the frame. OPEC and its wider OPEC+ grouping coordinate supply. The International Energy Agency, created after the 1973 embargo, holds strategic stock obligations and publishes the consuming world's demand view. The EIA supplies the US data everyone models from. And the price assessment agencies — Platts and Argus — determine what "the price" actually is for physical cargoes, which makes their methodology decisions quietly consequential.
14  /  The present moment

Where the industry stands in September 2026

This is a war year. Almost everything else in the market is downstream of one waterway.

Data as of 10 September 2026. Figures and events in this section were current at the v1.0 build and were not re-checked for v1.1; treat it as a dated snapshot. Claim-by-claim verification against primary sources is scheduled for the next major version.

1. Hormuz

A US-Israel military campaign against Iran began in late February 2026. Iran's response effectively closed the Strait of Hormuz, through which roughly a fifth of global oil and LNG supply passed in peacetime. Shipping largely stopped as insurers withdrew war-risk cover; Gulf exporters shut in production as regional storage filled. Brent went from about $72 the day before the strikes to $91 within a week, $109, and an intraday peak near $138 on 7 April — dated assessments briefly printed past $140, the highest since 2008. Total OPEC+ production fell from 42.77 million barrels a day in February to 33.13 million in May.

The subsequent six months have been a negotiation conducted through tanker attacks. A US-Iran memorandum on reopening the strait was signed on 17 June and collapsed within weeks over which routes vessels could use. Prices retraced to roughly $72 in early July on ceasefire optimism, then climbed steadily as talks stalled: $84 in mid-August, $89 by late August, and above $101 on 9 September — the first time since May — after the US destroyed five Iranian tankers, Houthi attacks forced temporary suspensions at Saudi facilities, and Ukrainian drones struck Novorossiysk, Russia's principal Black Sea terminal.

The EIA does not expect Middle East production to approach pre-conflict levels until early 2027 and forecasts Brent averaging around $90 in the second half of 2026, falling to $74 in 2027 as supply returns and inventories rebuild. Goldman Sachs carries $85 for December 2026 and warns that Brent could exceed $120 in 2027 if Gulf output stays four million barrels a day below prewar levels — explicitly not its base case. Global inventories have drawn by an estimated 400 million barrels this year. The US Strategic Petroleum Reserve is below 300 million barrels, its lowest since January 1983, which means the traditional Western policy response is largely spent.

Read every 2026 forecast with its date attached. The consensus for full-year Brent moved from the mid-$50s in mid-2025 to $90–100 by mid-2026. Goldman was at $56 for 2026 as recently as July 2025. This is not a criticism of forecasters; it is a demonstration that oil price forecasts are conditional on geopolitical assumptions that can invert in a week, and that any valuation resting on a long-run price deck should be tested against a wide range rather than defended.

2. OPEC is smaller than it was

The United Arab Emirates left OPEC effective 1 May 2026, taking with it a member accounting for more than 11% of the group's 2025 production and a substantial share of its spare capacity. Estimates put the reduction in OPEC spare capacity from around 3.8 to about 2.5 million barrels a day by 2027. The UAE has said it will raise output in the second half of the year and targets 5 million barrels a day of capacity by 2027.

The significance is structural rather than immediate. Spare capacity is the shock absorber that has damped oil price volatility for fifty years, and it is now both smaller and more concentrated in Saudi Arabia. A market with less buffer and one dominant holder of what remains has a fatter tail in both directions — which is exactly what 2026 has delivered.

3. American shale did not rescue the market

The traditional expectation — high prices bring US supply — worked partially and slowly. US output hit a record 13.93 million barrels a day in April 2026, up about 216,000 barrels from March, as Permian operators accelerated. But rig counts fell during periods when oil traded above $90, and Dallas Fed survey respondents were explicit about why: the price move was understood as a war premium that might vanish, and nobody wanted to commit multi-year capital to a two-week signal.

Underneath the volatility sits a slower structural problem. Tier 1 Permian acreage is maturing. The average Midland and Delaware operator saw roughly a 6% decline in oil productivity per well in 2025, with several large operators posting double-digit declines. Jefferies has forecast about 66,000 barrels a day of Permian growth for 2026 — nearly all of total US shale growth — with the Bakken and Eagle Ford in outright decline. The EIA expects Lower 48 onshore production to fall by roughly 170,000 barrels a day this year and Permian output to decline about 1% in 2027. The shale swing producer still swings, but with less amplitude than the market assumes.

4. Gas and LNG are on a different clock

Natural gas has been the calm half of the industry. The EIA's forecast has Henry Hub averaging around $3.46/MMBtu in 2026, roughly flat on 2025, then rising about a third to near $4.59 in 2027 as three major export facilities — Plaquemines, Corpus Christi Stage 3 and Golden Pass — ramp toward full operation and pull feedgas demand ahead of supply growth. US gas inventories are on track to end the injection season around 3,969 Bcf, about 5% above the five-year average.

The demand story underneath is new. US electricity sales are forecast at record levels in both 2026 and 2027, driven by data centre construction and industrial demand. Gas is the marginal fuel for much of that load. For the first time in a long while, the American gas thesis rests on domestic power demand as much as on exports.

5. Consolidation, then a pause

The 2023–25 merger wave — Exxon-Pioneer at roughly $60 billion, Chevron-Hess at $53 billion, Diamondback-Endeavor at $26 billion, ConocoPhillips-Marathon at $22.5 billion, Chesapeake-Southwestern forming Expand Energy — removed several major names from the market and left the Permian concentrated among a handful of operators. Deal volumes then fell for seven consecutive half-year periods, because those transactions raised asset prices and eliminated most of the attractive targets. Attention has shifted from Permian land to international long-life assets: Guyana, LNG supply, and offshore positions with two-decade horizons rather than two-year ones.

What this means for reading a company today. Three questions carry most of the weight. How much of its realised price is a war premium that could evaporate on a signed agreement? How deep is its remaining Tier 1 inventory, measured honestly at current spacing? And how much of its balance sheet strength was built during a period of prices that its own long-run deck does not assume?
15  /  Vocabulary

The words, defined

Filter by category or search. Oil jargon is dense, mostly precise, and occasionally deliberately vague — the glossary notes which is which.

Petroleum system Geology

The complete set of elements required for an accumulation: source rock, maturation, migration, reservoir, seal, trap and preservation. Missing any one produces nothing, which is why exploration success rates are low even in proven basins.

Kerogen Geology

The solid organic matter in a source rock from which hydrocarbons are generated on heating. Type I and II tend to yield oil, Type III mostly gas.

Oil window Geology

The temperature range, roughly 60–120°C, in which kerogen converts to liquid hydrocarbons. Cooler and it stays immature; hotter and you get gas, then nothing.

Porosity Geology

The fraction of a rock's volume that is void space capable of holding fluid. Storage capacity. A good reservoir might be 15–25% porous.

Permeability Geology

How readily fluid flows through connected pores, measured in darcies. Deliverability, and the thing that actually pays you. Shale permeability is measured in nanodarcies — billionths of a darcy.

Trap Geology

The geometry that stops migrating oil: an anticline, a fault block, a salt dome flank, a stratigraphic pinch-out, a reef. Structural traps are easier to image seismically; stratigraphic traps are subtler and often overlooked.

Seal / cap rock Geology

An impermeable barrier — shale, salt, anhydrite — above the reservoir that prevents further upward migration. Salt makes an exceptional seal, which is why so much of the world's oil sits near salt basins.

Source rock Geology

Organic-rich rock that generates hydrocarbons. In a shale play the source rock is also the reservoir, which is the defining feature of unconventional production.

Unconventional / resource play Geology

An accumulation that does not require a trap because the hydrocarbon never migrated. Continuous over large areas, requiring artificial permeability to produce. The Permian, Bakken, Eagle Ford, Marcellus, Vaca Muerta.

API gravity Geology

A density scale for crude, inverted so higher is lighter. Above about 31 is light, below 22 heavy. Light crude yields more high-value product from a simple refinery and therefore commands a premium.

Sweet and sour Geology

Low and high sulphur content, the threshold conventionally around 0.5%. Sour crude requires more refinery complexity to process and trades at a discount.

Condensate Geology

Very light hydrocarbon, gaseous in the reservoir and liquid at surface. Above roughly 45 API. Frequently sells below crude despite being lighter, because refinery configurations are not built for it.

1P / proved reserves Reserves

Quantities with reasonable certainty of recovery — conventionally at least a 90% probability of being met or exceeded. The only category permitted in SEC financial disclosure.

2P and 3P Reserves

Proved plus probable (50% probability) and proved plus probable plus possible (10% probability). The international and Canadian norm for 2P; 3P is an upside case that should not anchor a valuation.

PDP / PUD Reserves

Proved developed producing — a well exists and flows. Proved undeveloped — booked reserves requiring future capital. PUDs generally must be scheduled for drilling within five years of first booking.

12-month average price rule Reserves

The SEC requirement to book reserves at the unweighted average of first-day-of-month prices over the prior twelve months, held flat. Makes reported reserves partly a function of price history rather than geology.

PV-10 Reserves

Pre-tax present value of proved reserves discounted at 10% under SEC pricing rules. Comparable across filers and not a valuation. The after-tax version is the standardized measure.

PRMS Reserves

The SPE Petroleum Resources Management System — the international, probabilistic, forecast-price framework used outside SEC filings. Will give different numbers than SEC rules for the same asset.

Contingent resources Reserves

Discovered hydrocarbons without a current commercial path — no market, no infrastructure, no sanctioned development. Real oil, uncertain cash.

Reserve replacement ratio Reserves

Reserves added divided by production, in a year. Above 100% means booked inventory grew. Easily flattered by acquisitions and price revisions; insist on the organic, price-neutral figure.

Reserve life index Reserves

Reserves divided by annual production, expressed in years. A crude but useful check on whether a producer is running down its asset base.

EUR Reserves

Estimated ultimate recovery — total volume a well will produce over its life. Entirely dependent on the decline parameters assumed, particularly the b-factor and the terminal decline.

OOIP / STOOIP Reserves

Original oil in place: everything the reservoir contains, before any consideration of what can be extracted. Multiplied by the recovery factor to get recoverable volume. Quoting OOIP alone is a promotional tell.

Recovery factor Reserves

Recoverable volume as a share of oil in place. 20–35% for conventional under natural drive, 40–60% with secondary recovery, and only about 5–10% for shale.

Spud Drilling

To begin drilling a well. Spud-to-TD is the days taken to reach total depth, and it is the cleanest measure of drilling efficiency.

Lateral Drilling

The horizontal section of a well, drilled inside the target interval. Length drives both cost and productivity; 10,000 feet is standard and 15,000 or more increasingly common.

Completion Drilling

Everything done after drilling to make the well produce: perforating, hydraulic fracturing, installing production equipment. Now typically 55–70% of a shale well's total cost.

Hydraulic fracturing Drilling

Injecting fluid at pressure to crack the reservoir rock, with proppant to hold the fractures open. Creates the permeability that tight rock lacks.

Proppant Drilling

Sand or engineered ceramic pumped into fractures to keep them from closing. Loading per lateral foot is one of the main completion design variables.

Stage Drilling

An isolated section of the lateral treated separately during fracturing. Tighter stage spacing means more stages, more cost and generally better stimulation.

DUC Drilling

Drilled but uncompleted well. Cheap near-term production optionality because the drilling capital is already sunk. A falling DUC count flatters volumes without new capital.

Pad drilling Drilling

Multiple wells from one surface location. Lowers cost per well and enables simultaneous completions, at the cost of lumpier production timing.

Parent-child effect Drilling

Degradation of a new well's performance, and sometimes the existing well's, when drilled into rock already depleted by a neighbour. The central technical objection to dense-spacing inventory claims.

Frac hit Drilling

Pressure communication between a well being completed and a nearby producer, occasionally causing permanent damage. A real cost that appears in no standard cost metric.

Spacing Drilling

Distance between laterals and between stacked intervals. Determines claimed location counts, which determine inventory life, which drives valuation. Repeatedly revised wider after testing.

Workover Drilling

Intervention on an existing well to restore or improve production — replacing a pump, cleaning out scale, re-perforating. A recurring operating cost that rises with well age.

Decline curve Production

The falling profile of a well's production rate over time. The organising fact of the industry: it converts one capital decision into a fixed revenue schedule.

Arps equation Production

The 1945 formulation still used for most production forecasting, covering exponential, hyperbolic and harmonic decline through the parameters initial rate, initial decline and b-factor.

b-factor Production

The hyperbolic exponent controlling how fast the decline rate itself decelerates. Shale wells are commonly fitted between 0.8 and 1.5. Small increases add large amounts of claimed tail production, making this the most consequential contested number in shale valuation.

Terminal decline Production

A floor decline rate, usually 5–8% a year, imposed to stop a hyperbolic curve producing implausible or infinite recovery. Whether one is applied, and at what level, is worth asking of every type curve.

IP30 / IP90 Production

Average daily rate over the first 30 or 90 days. Far more informative than a 24-hour peak rate, which can be inflated by aggressive flowback at the cost of ultimate recovery.

Type curve Production

An average expected production profile for a class of wells, multiplied by a location count to build a corporate forecast. Usually fitted to the best wells drilled so far.

Base decline Production

The blended decline of a company's existing production. 6–10% for a conventional producer, 30–40% for a shale-heavy one. Sets the size of the reinvestment treadmill.

Water cut Production

Water as a share of total produced liquid. Rises through a conventional well's life until handling cost exceeds oil value and the well is shut in — usually the real end of a well, not exhaustion of oil.

GOR Production

Gas-oil ratio. Rises as reservoir pressure falls below the bubble point and dissolved gas breaks out. A rising GOR is a pressure-depletion signal and often precedes a rate decline.

Artificial lift Production

Pumps, gas lift or plunger systems installed when reservoir pressure can no longer push fluid to surface unaided. Nearly every mature well is on lift; the type and its failure rate drive operating cost.

Secondary and enhanced recovery Production

Waterflooding or gas injection to maintain reservoir pressure (secondary); CO₂, chemical or thermal methods to mobilise oil that pressure alone will not move (tertiary or EOR). Capital-intensive, and largely unsuccessful in shale so far.

Stripper well Production

A well producing under about 15 barrels a day. Collectively a meaningful share of US production, individually marginal, and the source of most orphan-well liability.

Brent / WTI / Dubai Midstream

The three principal crude benchmarks: North Sea waterborne, Cushing Oklahoma pipeline, and the medium sour barrel flowing east. Each is a specific grade at a specific place, not "oil".

Differential / basis Midstream

The gap between a local crude price and a benchmark, comprising transport cost, quality difference and takeaway scarcity. Blowouts in the differential are the market pricing a bottleneck.

Netback Midstream

Realised price less royalties, production taxes, transport and operating cost — the cash margin per barrel at the field. Excludes capital entirely.

Firm transportation Midstream

Contracted pipeline capacity paid for whether used or not. A genuine competitive asset when takeaway is scarce, and a fixed cost when it is not.

Crack spread Midstream

A refinery's margin: the value of the product slate minus the cost of crude. The 3-2-1 shorthand assumes three barrels of crude yielding two of gasoline and one of distillate.

Refinery complexity Midstream

The presence of upgrading units — cokers, hydrocrackers — that let a refinery buy cheap heavy sour crude and still produce high-value light products. Complexity converts the light-heavy spread into margin.

NGLs Midstream

Natural gas liquids: ethane, propane, butane, isobutane, natural gasoline, separated at a processing plant. Priced off their own markets, often quoted as a percentage of WTI.

Ethane rejection Midstream

Leaving ethane in the gas stream to be sold as fuel rather than extracting it as a liquid, when the liquid price does not justify processing. A swing of several hundred thousand barrels a day in reported US NGL supply.

Flaring Midstream

Burning associated gas that cannot be captured or moved. A visible waste, an emissions liability, and increasingly restricted by regulators and by investor pressure.

LNG Midstream

Natural gas chilled to liquid for shipment, which converts a regional commodity into a tradable one. Liquefaction capacity is the binding constraint and takes four to six years to build.

Half-cycle economics Economics

Drilling and completion cost against the revenue of one well. Excludes acreage, appraisal, overhead, interest, infrastructure and abandonment. Nearly every quoted breakeven is this.

Full-cycle economics Economics

Everything: land, exploration, infrastructure, corporate cost, financing and eventual plugging. The gap between the two explains how an industry of good wells produced a decade of poor returns.

F&D cost Economics

Finding and development cost per barrel of reserves added. Highly gameable through the denominator; use a three-year organic drill-bit average.

Recycle ratio Economics

Netback per barrel divided by F&D cost per barrel. Above 2.0x is healthy; below 1.0x means value is being destroyed on every barrel replaced.

Maintenance capital Economics

The capital needed to hold production flat against base decline. Free cash flow above maintenance capital is the number that matters, and it is rarely disclosed directly.

Reinvestment rate Economics

Capital spending as a share of operating cash flow. Above 100% is outspending; the post-2020 discipline consensus settled near 50–70%.

Payout Economics

Time for a well's cumulative net cash flow to equal its cost. Shale wells at healthy prices pay out in twelve to thirty months, which is what makes the half-cycle picture so attractive.

Inventory life Economics

Remaining drilling locations divided by annual drilling pace. Ten years of genuine Tier 1 supports a premium; three does not, regardless of current cash flow.

Working interest Finance

The share of a well's costs a party bears and the obligation to fund development. The only interest that can elect to deduct intangible drilling costs.

Net revenue interest Finance

Working interest multiplied by one minus the royalty burden. A 100% working interest under a 25% royalty is a 75% NRI. Working interest is your share of the bill; NRI is your share of the cheque.

Royalty interest Finance

A share of production revenue free of all drilling and operating cost, retained by the mineral owner. Historically one-eighth; now often 20–25% in competitive basins.

Overriding royalty (ORRI) Finance

A royalty carved out of the working interest rather than the mineral estate, commonly granted to the person who assembled the deal. Expires with the lease.

Reserve-based lending Finance

Bank credit sized to a borrowing base derived from discounted proved reserves, redetermined semi-annually at the banks' own price deck. Procyclical by construction — the facility shrinks exactly when cash flow does.

Farmout Finance

An acreage holder gives a partner an interest in exchange for the partner funding drilling. The classic structure for a company with land and no capital.

Carried interest Finance

An interest whose costs are paid by another party, usually until payout. The mechanism inside most farmouts and DrillCo arrangements.

JOA Finance

Joint operating agreement. Governs who operates a well, how costs are billed, and what happens when a partner declines to fund — the non-consent penalty, which can run to several hundred percent before the interest reverts.

Held by production Finance

A lease clause continuing the lease indefinitely so long as production continues. Explains why operators sometimes drill uneconomic wells: losing the acreage costs more.

MLP Finance

Master limited partnership. Avoids entity-level tax by distributing nearly all cash flow, issuing K-1s. Suits fee-based midstream; suits volatile upstream production badly.

Volumetric production payment Finance

Selling a fixed volume of future production for cash today. Legally a sale of a real property interest rather than a loan, with consequences in bankruptcy.

Asset retirement obligation Finance

The discounted liability for plugging wells and restoring sites. Frequently understated, and a growing public burden as marginal wells change hands to ever-smaller owners.

Concession / royalty-tax regime Fiscal

The company owns the hydrocarbons at the wellhead; the state takes a royalty on gross production and taxes the profit. The US, Canada, UK, Norway, Australia and much of Latin America.

Production sharing contract Fiscal

The state retains ownership; the contractor recovers costs from "cost oil" up to a ceiling and splits the remaining "profit oil" with the state. Dominant across Asia, Africa and the Middle East.

Cost oil and profit oil Fiscal

The two portions of PSC production. The cost recovery ceiling limits how fast a contractor can recoup spending; unrecovered costs carry forward. The ceiling is often more economically important than the headline profit split.

R-factor Fiscal

Cumulative contractor revenue divided by cumulative contractor cost, used to slide the profit oil split progressively toward the state as a project pays back. A common feature of modern PSCs.

Government take Fiscal

The state's share of divisible income — revenue less recoverable cost — across all fiscal instruments combined. The standard basis for comparing regimes, and it ranges from roughly 40% to over 90%.

Service contract Fiscal

The contractor is paid a fee per barrel with no price exposure. Iraq's post-2009 technical service contracts are the largest example. Low risk, low reward.

Intangible drilling costs Fiscal

The non-salvageable portion of a well — labour, rig time, fluids, cementing, services — typically 60–85% of cost, deductible in full in the year incurred by working interest owners under IRC §263(c).

Percentage depletion Fiscal

A deduction of 15% of gross income from a property, available to independent producers and royalty owners subject to net income and production limits. Not capped at capital invested.

Severance tax Fiscal

A state tax on the value of production at the wellhead. Texas charges 4.6% on oil and 7.5% on gas; combined with county ad valorem tax the burden commonly runs 6–9% of revenue.

Ring-fencing Fiscal

Treating each licence or field as a separate taxpayer so losses in one cannot shelter profits in another. Materially raises effective take and discourages exploration by established producers.

Successful efforts vs full cost Fiscal

Two permitted accounting methods. Successful efforts expenses dry holes immediately; full cost capitalises them into a pool subject to a ceiling test that forces write-downs when prices fall.

OPEC and OPEC+ Market

The producer group that coordinates supply, and its wider grouping including Russia and others. Its withheld capacity has damped price volatility for fifty years — a function now diminished by the UAE's 2026 departure.

Spare capacity Market

Production that can be brought online within about 90 days and sustained. The market's shock absorber. Concentrated in Saudi Arabia, and smaller now than at any point in years.

Contango and backwardation Market

Futures above spot, and below it. Contango pays you to store; backwardation signals physical tightness and penalises inventory. 2026 has been persistently backwardated.

Strategic Petroleum Reserve Market

Government-held emergency crude stocks. The US reserve is below 300 million barrels as of August 2026, its lowest since January 1983, which limits the traditional policy response to a supply shock.

Strait of Hormuz Market

The waterway between Iran and Oman carrying roughly a fifth of global oil and LNG in peacetime. Effectively closed since February 2026, and the single largest determinant of the current price.

Boe Market

Barrel of oil equivalent, converting gas at 6,000 cubic feet per barrel. Roughly right on energy content and badly wrong on value. Always demand the liquids split.

Price deck Market

The set of forward commodity prices used in a model or a bank's borrowing base determination. Two analysts disagreeing about a company usually disagree about the deck, not the asset.

Hedging Market

Fixing forward prices with swaps, collars or puts. Protects a drilling programme and satisfies lenders, and makes realised price a treasury outcome rather than an operating one. Strip it out before comparing companies.

16  /  What to retain

Twelve things worth keeping

If everything else evaporates, these survive.

1

The decline curve governs

Production rate is set by reservoir physics, not by management. One capital decision produces a fixed revenue schedule that falls from day one.

2

Shale is front-loaded

Roughly half of a shale well's lifetime output arrives in its first three years. That shape drives the financing, the accounting and the disappointments.

3

The b-factor is where the tail lives

Raising the hyperbolic exponent barely changes the near term and transforms the estimated ultimate recovery. Interrogate it, and the terminal decline beside it.

4

Half-cycle is not full-cycle

Nearly every quoted breakeven excludes land, overhead, interest, infrastructure and abandonment. The gap is what turned a decade of good wells into poor returns.

5

Reserves move with price rules

The SEC's twelve-month average pricing makes reported reserves partly a price history. Read the revisions line in the reconciliation table, not the headline.

6

The screen price is not your price

Differentials, quality adjustments, gathering, royalty and severance tax can take more than half of the benchmark before a dollar of capital is recovered.

7

Boe hides the mix

Gas converts at 6:1 on energy and something closer to 20:1 on value. Growth in boe with a shifting mix can be shrinkage in revenue.

8

Inventory quality is the asset

Current production is visible and temporary. What determines value is how many genuinely good locations remain, at spacing that has actually been tested.

9

Working interest pays; NRI receives

Confusing the two overstates cash flow by the whole royalty burden, and it happens in real models.

10

The fiscal regime is half the deal

Government take ranges from around 40% to over 90%. A cost recovery ceiling can matter more to a contractor's return than the headline profit split.

11

The tax code is deliberate policy

Expensing intangible drilling costs and 15% percentage depletion are designed to lower the effective cost of capital for domestic drilling. They change behaviour, not just tax bills.

12

The price forecast is the valuation

Consensus 2026 Brent moved from the mid-$50s to $90–100 in under a year on geopolitics. Any single-price valuation is a bet on a forecast that has repeatedly inverted.

The question to ask first, always

What does this company have to do next year just to stay the same size? Base decline times production, divided by the first-year output of a typical new well, times well cost. Compare the answer to operating cash flow. Everything above that line is available to owners; everything below it means the business is shrinking or borrowing. Most oil disappointments are visible in that single calculation years before they appear in the share price.
